PROJECT 04

The Flute Cavity: A High-Q Microwave Cavity for Storing Scattered Photons

ROLE: [your role] TOOLS: SolidWorks, Ansys HFSS, Fusion, Haas CNC mill TIMEFRAME: Summer 2026

The flute cavity is a high-Q microwave cavity built to catch photons scattered by defects in a silicon sample and hold on to them, so their frequencies can be read out at a later time. We designed, simulated, and machined it in Fitz Lab, Mattias Fitzpatrick’s quantum technologies research lab at Dartmouth, together with Sam Bryan.

Problem

We are probing a silicon sample with microwave photons and watching for scattering from two-level system (TLS) defects. We were challenged to design and build a high-Q cavity for the scattered photons, where their electromagnetic frequencies can be stored and read out at a later time.

Constraints: one summer, and a final part that has to be cooled to 7 millikelvin inside a dilution refrigerator. That puts a hard limit on size, which turned out to shape most of the design.

How it works

The setup pairs a low-Q cavity with the high-Q flute cavity, coupled through the silicon sample. Step through the sequence below: drive the sample, switch the drive off, then read out what the flute cavity stored.

Approach

Design process

  1. Starting from first principles

    We began with the electric and magnetic field wave equations.

    ∇2E=μ0ε0∂2E∂t2∇2B=μ0ε0∂2B∂t2

    We assume a separable solution for the electric field,

    E(x,y,z,t)=X(x)Y(y)Z(z)T(t)

    and apply the boundary condition that the electric field parallel to a metal wall must vanish:

    E∥=0at the metal walls

    The boundary conditions require standing-wave solutions, so the allowed wave numbers are

    kx=nπaky=mπbkz=lπd

    and therefore

    k2=(nπa)2+(mπb)2+(lπd)2

    Since ω = ck and ω = 2πf, we get the cavity frequency expression

    fnml=c2(na)2+(mb)2+(ld)2

  2. Tuning the mode families with geometry

    The frequency equation gives rise to families of modes. For a 130 × 5.4 × 1.5 cm cavity there are ten families and 1096 modes between 1 and 15 GHz, far too crowded to use directly.

    The cavity dimensions let us pick which families matter:

    • Small Z dimension pushes the l mode families to higher frequencies, so they become irrelevant.
    • Y dimension controls mode spacing.
    • X dimension controls the base frequency.

    Our solution was to grab the first mode family, (n, m, l) = (i, 1, 0), so there are no other families of modes to worry about, then adjust X and Y to get the desired frequency range and mode spacing. The remaining problem: modes within that family are not linearly spaced. The fix was to use a taper in the geometry.

  3. A tapered first model

    A quadratic taper was added to the Y dimension of the holes of the flute cavity to achieve linear spacing of the resonant modes. After solving analytically for the required geometry, we built the first model in SolidWorks: 2.8–5.2 GHz with 100 MHz linear spacing, in a 130 cm × 5.4 cm × 1.5 cm cavity. We then took the SolidWorks design into Ansys HFSS to simulate the modes.

    The model simulated as we expected. The numerical and analytical solutions agreed, which gave us more confidence in the design.

  4. Folding it to fit the fridge

    The final result has to be cooled to 7 millikelvin in a refrigerator, and a design about a meter long is far too large. We needed a way to make it smaller, so we started a new design to see whether the hole pattern could be folded. In simulation there was no noticeable difference between the folded and straight versions.

  5. Checking that the holes are sealed

    We also checked the depth of the holes to make sure they were “sealed.” Each drilled hole acts as a tiny circular waveguide with a cutoff frequency set by its diameter:

    fc=1.841cπd

    Since the cavity modes sit below this cutoff, the field cannot propagate up the hole. It decays exponentially along the hole’s depth (the “stem”), and the length of the stem determines the Q: how well sealed the cavity is.

  6. The final design

    Putting it together gave the final design: 150 MHz spacing with 0.8 cm diameter holes, in both a straight and a folded layout (the renderings are at the top of this page). The HFSS field plots show the lowest modes spreading along the row of holes in each.

  7. Learning to machine it

    With the design finished, we had to realize the part on a CNC machine. The approach follows the deep-drilling and cavity-opening method in Phys. Rev. Lett. 127, 107701 (2021).

    Before handing anything to the machine, I made a few test cavities on the manual end mill, where everything is controlled by hand, so I could feel the stress the drill was under. That told me what feed rates to program on the Haas mill. We had to be very careful and use peck drilling to keep clearing aluminum chips from the hole, because the hole depth was right at the limit of the depth-to-diameter rating for the drill bit.

  8. A test cavity on the end mill

    Using the end mill, we made a simplified test cavity to answer our biggest worry before committing to the final part: whether the drill holes were deep enough for the evanescent seals to work. It was a crude manufacturing process, made with only drill bits and not cleaned or honed on the inside. Tools were still on order, and metal flakes were still left inside.

    We did observe mode resonances in the test cavity, which relieved our largest worry: the drill holes being too shallow and the evanescent seals not working.

    Takeaways

    The flute cavity method does support resonant modes. The modes are not exactly where the simulation said they would be (our hypothesis: flakes left inside the cavity from drilling may contribute). The placement of the SMA pins is very important and very sensitive.
