PROJECT 02

Shadow Spine: A Wearable Posture Monitor for Squats and Deadlifts

TOOLS: SolidWorks, 3D printing, Arduino, soldering TIMEFRAME: Fall 2025 (10 weeks)

Shadow Spine is a low-cost wearable that teaches safe squat and deadlift form by measuring the curve of your back in real time and giving haptic feedback when you leave a neutral spine. Built for Dartmouth's Introduction to Engineering with Starlone Ekuwom, Daniel Smith, and Jake Romney.

#1voted best in class
6potentiometer-based joints
~1°max difference vs. protractor
3full prototype generations

Problem

Personal trainers are one of the best ways to learn good lifting form, and one of the most expensive. Most people learning to squat or deadlift get no feedback at all, and the lower and middle spine is the area most vulnerable to lifting injuries. Our question: is there a less expensive way to learn good squat and deadlift form in the gym?

Constraints: a 10-week term, a student budget, and a device that had to be comfortable, easy to use mid-lift, and reasonably easy to build.

Approach

Choosing a concept

We brainstormed solutions and wrote out the specifications we wanted: cost, comfort, ease of use, effectiveness, safety, and more. Then we scored each idea (changing the workout, hiring a trainer, a lifting buddy, a training belt, lighter weight, and a wearable spine monitor) in a weighted decision matrix. Shadow Spine had the best weighted score on our scale and became the concept we pursued.

Design process

  1. Sketch and cardboard mockup

    We started with a hand sketch of a vest with a row of linkages that move in unison with the spine, then built a quick cardboard-and-strap version to see how a linked "spine" would lie and flex on a back.

  2. Proving the sensing idea

    To measure bending, each side of a linkage joint connects to opposite sides of a potentiometer, so as the joint bends, resistance changes and the Arduino reads a different voltage. A first prototype used a potentiometer in a hinge to confirm the idea worked, and we drew up a wiring schematic for chaining six of them together.

  3. Custom housing in SolidWorks

    The hinge worked, but it was far too bulky to wear. We designed a custom link in SolidWorks that holds the potentiometer securely, allows the full range of motion between links, sits flat against the back, and leaves a channel for wiring. One link was 3D printed and tested next to the hinge before we committed to printing a chain.

  4. First multi-link spine, and a quality problem

    Chaining the links gave us a full proof of concept. The user stands in a neutral spine and presses a "zero" button so the Arduino records each sensor's neutral reading. Any deviation beyond a set angle triggers a warning. Early on, though, readings drifted: after bending and returning to neutral, the voltage often didn't come back to where it started. The cause was loose wipers inside the potentiometers. We rebuilt the system and quality-checked each part, and about 70% of the off-the-shelf potentiometers had too much play to use.

  5. Shrinking it down

    We sketched a final system: a thin spine strip attached to a shirt with Velcro, a waist strap to hold it tight, and a small control box at the hip. We printed several compact link designs in different colors and compared them, and we settled on the orange version. To make it fit, we cut down the potentiometers and used the thickest wire we could still solder.

  6. Better feedback, smaller electronics

    In testing, we found LEDs were hard to notice while concentrating on a lift. We replaced them with a haptic motor that vibrates against the back during poor posture and stops only once form is corrected. We also moved from a full Arduino to an Arduino Nano on a small solderable breadboard so everything fits in a hip-mounted box.

Result

The finished device is a wearable chain of six sensing links, a waist strap, and a hip controller. The wearer zeroes it in neutral spine, lifts, and feels a vibration if their back rounds past the threshold.

Testing

We first validated accuracy by comparing the Arduino's angle readings against a protractor across six trials per sensor. Averages agreed to within roughly 1° at every joint, with tight spread between trials. We then took the device to the gym and tested with real lifters. Feedback was largely positive.

Recognition

Our project was voted best in the class and received the Philip R. Jackson Award for excellence in engineering from Dartmouth's Thayer School of Engineering.

What I'd Do Differently

Fit was the biggest issue lifters raised: a single-size prototype doesn't fit everyone. With more time I would make the spine adjustable in length (or offer sizes) and test with a wider range of body types earlier. I'd also screen components from day one: finding out late that most of our potentiometers were too loose cost us a rebuild, and a quick incoming-parts check would have saved it.
